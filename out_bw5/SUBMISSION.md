## Part 2 -- Framework & Holistic Synthesis

The global landscape is rapidly coalescing around a new era of **modern mercantilism, fundamentally reshaped by the accelerating advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI).** Governments worldwide, recognizing AI as a critical determinant of future national power and economic dominance, are actively intervening to control its development, deployment, and associated supply chains. This is not a subtle shift; it is a **"return with a vengeance"** of industrial policy, with an overwhelming **183 of the world's 195 countries actively targeting at least one industry** (E01). This pervasive state intervention is creating a bifurcated global environment: one characterized by aggressive state-sponsored AI development and resource acquisition, and another facing increasing friction and strategic control by dominant AI-producing nations.

The core of this new mercantilism lies in the strategic imperative to secure and control the foundational elements of AI, most critically advanced semiconductor technology. The US, in particular, is enacting policies designed to maintain its leadership. Draft export control regulations, for instance, outline tiered frameworks that differentiate between simple reviews for smaller GPU shipments and complex governmental agreements requiring local investments for larger exports (E04). This is not merely about preventing rivals from accessing cutting-edge technology; it's about **forcing a quid pro quo that benefits American industry and national security.** Similarly, the US Department of Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has moved to a case-by-case review for high-end AI chips (E02), signaling a deliberate and granular approach to managing access to critical AI hardware. These actions are directly aimed at **"tightening verification requirements for authorized semiconductor locations and enhancing multilateral coordination of technology export controls"** (E08), demonstrating a clear intent to manage the flow of AI-enabling technologies.

This aggressive nationalistic approach is further evidenced by the US administration's explicit targeting of fragmented state-level regulations in December 2025 to **"consolidate a unified national policy framework for artificial intelligence"** (E06). This move underscores the perception of AI as a matter of national security and economic survival, requiring a centralized, top-down strategy. The emergence of initiatives like OpenAI's 'Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age' (E03), which received over 400 policy responses and offers significant research grants and API credits, indicates a broader ecosystem embracing and actively shaping industrial policy within the AI domain. While details of specific recipients are unknown, the sheer volume of engagement signifies a widespread belief in the efficacy and necessity of state-guided industrial development in AI.

The economic implications of this trend are profound. Over the past decade leading up to August 2026, global venture investment in AI has exploded **thirty-fold, from $8.3 billion to $259 billion** (E05). This massive influx of capital, while impressive, is increasingly being channeled and influenced by state-led industrial policy. Governments are not just facilitating private investment; they are actively directing it towards strategic national goals, potentially crowding out purely market-driven innovation. As highlighted by Bridgewater Associates in April 2026, the confluence of modern mercantilism and AI infrastructure demands has **"accelerated acute supply-demand mismatches and resource grabs in critical physical commodities"** (E07). This implies a fierce competition for the raw materials and manufacturing capabilities necessary to build AI, exacerbating geopolitical tensions and creating new supply chain vulnerabilities.

The framework for understanding this intersection is therefore one of **strategic competition and managed scarcity**. Nations are not merely competing in the marketplace; they are actively engaged in a geopolitical struggle to control the future of AI. The dominant powers, particularly the US, are using their existing technological and economic leverage to set the terms of engagement. This involves not only regulating exports of advanced hardware but also incentivizing domestic production and R&D, and potentially imposing conditions on foreign investment and collaboration. The "case-by-case review" (E02) and tiered export frameworks (E04) are not merely bureaucratic processes; they are tools of geopolitical leverage designed to extract concessions and maintain a technological advantage. Countries that fail to align with these dominant power structures risk being relegated to the periphery of the AI revolution, dependent on technologies and policies dictated by others.

This is a zero-sum game where national advantage is paramount. The "return with a vengeance" of industrial policy (E01) is a clear signal that governments view AI as a vital strategic asset, akin to oil or advanced weaponry in previous eras. The massive growth in AI venture investment (E05) is not a purely organic phenomenon; it is being steered and shaped by these industrial policies. The resulting resource grabs (E07) are direct consequences of this state-driven competition. Ultimately, the framework for understanding modern mercantilism and AI is one where **nations are aggressively pursuing self-sufficiency and dominance in AI, employing industrial policy as their primary weapon to control supply chains, foster domestic innovation, and gain a decisive geopolitical advantage.** The era of unfettered global AI development is over; we are now in an era of managed, nationalized AI, dictated by the strategic interests of powerful states.
